Bone dust

2022-10-03 2 min read (309 words)

The Nobel for medicine went this morning to Svante Pääbo, who worked out how to read the DNA of people who died forty thousand years ago, and I have thought about very little else since about eight.

Here’s the bit that got me, because it isn’t the bit you’d expect.

You’d assume the hard part is that the DNA is old and broken, and it is, it’s in tiny fragments with no instructions attached. But the properly horrible problem is contamination. Bone that old is absolutely full of bacteria, and it’s been handled by archaeologists and curators and lab technicians for decades, and every one of those people is a modern human whose DNA looks an awful lot like the thing you’re hunting for.

So you go looking for a human sequence in an ancient bone and you find one straight away, every time, and the overwhelming likelihood is that it’s yours.

Which means the reading turns out to be a fairly small part of it, and most of the decades went on building a way to prove that what you’ve read is genuinely old. Clean rooms, controls, chemical fingerprints of real age, statistics to catch a fragment that walked in on somebody’s glove. All of that, so that when the answer finally arrives anybody has any reason at all to believe it.

And then the answer arrives, and it’s that one to four percent of the DNA in most people alive outside Africa came from Neanderthals. Which rather changes what the word “extinct” is doing in that sentence.

I love that the prize is really for the not-fooling-yourself. That’s the whole job, in every field I’ve ever been near. Getting a result is easy. Getting a result you’re allowed to trust takes thirty years.

Anyway. Go and read about it. Much nicer than the rest of today’s news.
